## Atomic design

Norte follows the idea behind [atomic design](https://atomicdesign.bradfrost.com/chapter-2/), by Brad Frost. It
describes an interface the way chemistry describes matter: atoms join into molecules, molecules into organisms, and
organisms fill templates and pages.

What matters is the direction. Big pieces are made from small, finished ones, and each piece can be designed, tested
and reused on its own before it's part of something bigger. A fix to a button reaches every dialog, form and page that
uses it.

Norte keeps the idea and drops the chemistry names. Nobody should have to argue whether a menu is a molecule or an
organism: the names say what a piece is for.

## The layers

From the smallest to the largest, in the order of the sidebar.

1. **Foundations, the tokens.** Colors, type, spacing, radius, elevation, motion and the icon set. They draw nothing on
   their own, but every value on screen comes from one. In atomic design, they'd sit below the atoms.
2. **Elements, the atoms.** One job each: a button, a text field, a badge. They know nothing about the product.
3. **Components, the molecules and organisms.** Elements put together to do something bigger: a dialog, a menu, tabs, a
   card. Norte doesn't split molecules from organisms: the line between them is blurry, and size isn't what matters.
   What matters is that a component still knows nothing about the product.
4. **AI and Data, the product's own organisms.** Components that know Norte's world: a run timeline, an approval
   request, a line chart. This is where the product's language lives.
5. **Pages, the templates and pages.** Whole screens of the workspace, built only from the layers above, with realistic
   content.

## Rules

- **Build only on the layers below.** An element never uses a component, and a component never uses a piece of AI. If a
  piece needs something from a layer above, it's in the wrong layer.
- **Compose, don't configure.** When a component starts growing a prop for every case, split it into parts that fit
  together (a card, its header, its actions) instead.
- **Layout belongs to the parent.** Pieces take no outside margin; whoever places them sets the gap, with a spacing
  token. That's what lets the same button sit in a toolbar, a dialog and a form.
- **States are stories, not pieces.** Loading, empty, error and disabled are stories of their component, never
  components of their own.
- **Each piece has one home.** It lives in one layer, in one group. A specific use stays with its domain: an approval
  dialog is built on Dialog, and lives in AI.

## Where a piece belongs

Ask these in order. The first question that sends the piece somewhere decides its layer.

1. **Does it know about agents, runs or approvals?** Then it lives in AI, however small it is (a run's status). Charts
   and tables live in Data.
2. **Does it arrange other pieces?** Groups, lists, stacks, and anything with room for other pieces inside it, are
   components.
3. **Does it open, close or control something else?** A tab and its panel, a trigger and its menu: components.
4. **Is it made of pieces that each keep a job of their own?** Then it's a component. A search that suggests results
   as you type is a text field that takes the query and a list that offers matches: two jobs.
5. **None of the above?** It does one job and would work the same in any product: it's an element.

Holding another element doesn't make a piece a component on its own. An icon button has an icon inside, but the icon
has no job there: it's the button's label, so the icon button is an element. A text field's clear button only empties
the field, which is still the field's one job, editing its text: the text field stays an element. A toolbar of icon
buttons arranges them and moves focus between them: a component.

> **Not sure?** Build it as a component. A piece that turns out to have one job moves down to Elements easily; an
> element that grew a layout is harder to take apart, because everything that uses it depends on that layout.
